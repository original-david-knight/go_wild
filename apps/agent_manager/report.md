# US-Iran Conflict: Status of Tensions, Nuclear Talks, and Ceasefire Prospects

## Active War Since February 28, 2026

The US and Israel launched joint strikes against Iran on February 28, 2026, under the US codename "Operation Epic Fury," targeting nuclear facilities, ballistic missile infrastructure, and senior leadership. Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei was killed in the opening strikes; his son Mojtaba Khamenei was elected as successor on March 8. As of Day 18 of the conflict, US Central Command reports over 7,000 targets struck. Documented casualties include at least 5,300 dead in Iran (511 civilians, 4,789 military per Hengaw), 13 US service members, 17 in Israel, and over 880 in Lebanon from expanded Israeli strikes. Thirteen Iranian health infrastructure sites have been damaged, along with UNESCO-listed heritage sites at Isfahan's Naqsh-e Jahan Square. Iran responded with over 500 ballistic missiles and 2,000 drones since February 28, though launch rates have collapsed over 90% -- from 350 missiles/day on Day 1 to roughly 25 by Day 15.

## Nuclear Program and IAEA Access

Prior to the war, Iran held 440.9 kg of uranium enriched to 60% purity -- a short technical step from weapons-grade 90%. The IAEA currently has no access to any of Iran's four declared enrichment facilities due to the conflict. While satellite imagery has not detected renewed enrichment activity, the agency cannot verify whether Iran has suspended enrichment or confirm the size of remaining stockpiles. On March 18, IAEA Director General Grossi urged both sides to return to negotiations, stating that nuclear concerns "cannot be solved by military action alone."

## Strait of Hormuz Closure and Economic Fallout

On March 4, Iran declared the Strait of Hormuz closed to US, Israeli, and allied shipping using targeted drone strikes that drove insurers and shipping firms out of the waterway. This disrupted roughly 20% of global daily oil supply. Brent crude breached $100/barrel on March 8 for the first time in four years, peaking at $126. Iran has selectively allowed passage for Indian and Saudi-flagged vessels. The US military has acknowledged it is "not ready" to escort commercial shipping through the strait at scale. Trump has publicly pressured NATO and China to assist in reopening Hormuz.

## Collapse of the February Diplomatic Track

Indirect US-Iran nuclear negotiations, mediated by Oman, had produced a near-breakthrough by February 27. Oman's Foreign Minister announced Iran had agreed to forgo nuclear material stockpiling and accept full IAEA verification, calling peace "within reach." The February 28 strikes obliterated this diplomatic track. Arms Control Association analysts have criticized US negotiators as "ill-prepared for serious nuclear negotiations," suggesting the military option was preferred over the diplomatic path that was materializing. Trump's maximum pressure campaign -- reinstated in February 2025 with sanctions driving Iranian inflation to 40% and food inflation to 70% -- had created the conditions for Iran's willingness to negotiate, but the administration chose escalation over the deal on the table.

## Current Ceasefire Dynamics

Both sides publicly reject ceasefire talks while backchannel communications have resumed. Axios reported on March 16 that US envoy Steve Witkoff and Iranian FM Abbas Araghchi exchanged text messages -- the first direct contact since the war began -- though both sides dispute who initiated and Iran formally denies any contact occurred. Iran's official position, stated by Araghchi: "We never asked for a ceasefire... we are ready to defend ourselves as long as it takes." He has demanded not a temporary ceasefire but a permanent end to hostilities "on all fronts" across the Middle East, including Lebanon. Trump stated on March 15 that "Iran wants to make a deal, and I don't want to make it because the terms aren't good enough yet," indicating he seeks Iran's complete abandonment of nuclear ambitions as a precondition. Trump has also rejected intermediary attempts by Middle Eastern allies to broker talks.

## Likelihood of Ceasefire by June 30, 2026

A formal ceasefire agreement by June 30 faces severe obstacles but cannot be ruled out. **Factors favoring a deal:** Iran's missile launch rate has collapsed over 90%, suggesting rapidly depleting military capacity. The Hormuz closure is inflicting global economic pain that creates pressure on all parties. Backchannel communications are active despite public denials. **Factors against:** Both leaders have staked domestic credibility on not backing down -- Iran demands permanent security guarantees and an end to operations in Lebanon; Trump demands total nuclear capitulation. The assassination of Khamenei makes Iranian concessions politically toxic for the new leadership. The International Crisis Group has warned that without an off-ramp, the conflict risks further regional escalation into a multi-front war. **Assessment:** The military trajectory (Iran's degraded capacity, US operational costs, global economic disruption from Hormuz) creates structural incentives for a cessation of hostilities within 2-3 months, but a comprehensive, formalized agreement addressing nuclear issues by June 30 remains unlikely. A narrower tactical ceasefire or de-escalation framework brokered through Oman or a third party is the more plausible near-term outcome.
